## The question

The literature offers two competing pictures of how MoO<sub>3</sub> loses activity during low-pressure hydrodeoxygenation. One blames **over-reduction**: active Mo<sup>6+</sup> and Mo<sup>5+</sup> species are reduced to Mo<sup>4+</sup> in MoO<sub>2</sub>, which cannot form the oxygen vacancies the reaction needs. The other proposes that a **metastable oxycarbohydride phase**, MoO<sub>x</sub>C<sub>y</sub>H<sub>z</sub>, forms during reaction and protects the catalyst from that over-reduction. Most of that evidence came from single model compounds and short runs. Real lignin vapor contains many oxygenates, and long-run behavior is what decides whether a process is viable.

## What I did

- Ran **long time-on-stream experiments** with three lignin-derived model compounds, an aromatic ether (anisole) and two ketones (acetone and 3-pentanone), at several temperatures, to see how the reactant and the conditions shape deactivation.
- Characterized **fresh, reduced and spent catalysts** with powder XRD, XPS, TGA-MS and temperature-programmed oxidation, elemental analysis and SEM, to connect the kinetics to changes in the crystal phases, oxidation states, carbon content and morphology.
- Tested **regeneration** by air calcination, and several **pretreatments**: exposing the catalyst to one oxygenate before another, and synthesizing a deliberately carburized oxycarbide catalyst.

## What I found so far

- **Deactivation depends on what you feed.** How fast the catalyst loses activity depends on the oxygenate and on temperature, and the activity kept falling over long runs instead of levelling off.
- **The oxycarbohydride phase is not the stabilizer it was thought to be.** It forms with every oxygenate tested and holds most of the carbon on the catalyst, yet the catalyst still deactivates.
- **Oxidation state alone does not explain it.** Ex situ XPS did not track deactivation with the Mo oxidation state. Because the samples had to be passivated before analysis, the next step is air-free XPS.
- **The catalyst is fully regenerable.** Calcining in air restores the activity, but the productivity lost on stream and during regeneration still makes a more robust catalyst necessary.
- **Carbon in the lattice looks like the lever.** Pretreatment results suggest that controlling, not maximizing, how much carbon enters the MoO<sub>3</sub> lattice is key to stability.

## What comes next

- **Air-free characterization:** isolating spent catalysts with air-tight valves and transferring them to XPS inside a glove box, so the surface is measured as it was in the reactor.
- **Atomic-scale imaging:** high-resolution TEM on spent MoO<sub>3</sub> nanosheets to see the oxycarbidic phase and the lattice distortion caused by carbon uptake.
- **Controlled carbon:** synthesizing a phase-pure oxycarbohydride and catalysts with set amounts of lattice carbon, to isolate how carbon affects stability.

## Why it matters for industry

Measured deactivation behavior and a proven regeneration protocol feed directly into **cycle-time and swing-reactor design**. Understanding the role of lattice carbon points toward a pretreatment or formulation that keeps the catalyst on stream longer, which is what ultimately sets the process economics.
